Concept 01

Weights — the Machine's Knobs

Imagine you're a cricket commentator trying to predict the final score of a T20 innings. You look at two things: the number of overs remaining and the number of wickets in hand. Without knowing the exact formula, you might instinctively say:

Predicted runs ≈ overs remaining × some-number + wickets in hand × some-other-number

Those two "some-numbers" are exactly what machine learning calls weights. They are adjustable dials inside a prediction machine. The machine multiplies each input by its weight and adds everything up to make a prediction.

Think of mixing a fresh mango juice at a juice stall. You add mango pulp and sugar. How much of each makes the perfect glass? The "spoons of pulp per glass" and the "spoons of sugar per glass" are your weights. Too much sugar and it's cloying. Too little pulp and it tastes watery. The recipe — those two amounts — is exactly what the machine must learn. The weights are the knowledge.

The Formula

Prediction = Input₁ × w₁ + Input₂ × w₂

Here, w₁ (weight 1) and w₂ (weight 2) are what the machine needs to learn. The inputs are things we measure (height, length, price, temperature — whatever is relevant). The prediction is what we want to estimate.

Limitation 2: We only have two inputs here. Real models may have thousands of inputs (think: every word in a sentence). With more inputs you need more weights, and finding the right combination gets exponentially harder.
You could! For 2 weights it might work with effort. But real neural networks have billions of weights. GPT-4 has roughly 1.8 trillion parameters (weights). No human can tune those by hand — the machine must learn them automatically. That's exactly what the next concepts teach us.
We now know the machine makes predictions using weights. But how do we know if those predictions are any good? We need a way to measure the quality of our guesses — and that measurement is called Loss.
Concept 02

Loss — Measuring How Wrong You Are

Every time the machine makes a prediction, it might be a bit too high or a bit too low. The loss is a single number that captures how wrong the machine was — across all its predictions combined. Lower loss = better predictions. A loss of zero = perfect predictions.

🎯 Analogy You run a shop and you guess daily sales: Day 1 you guess ₹500, actual is ₹600 (off by −₹100). Day 2 you guess ₹700, actual is ₹400 (off by +₹300). Just adding the errors: −100 + 300 = 200. But that makes Day 1's error look like it partly cancels Day 2's. Squaring the errors — 100² + 300² = 10,000 + 90,000 = 1,00,000 — punishes big mistakes more and treats over- and under-guessing equally.

How Loss is Calculated

Error = Prediction − Actual
Squared Error = Error²
Loss = Sum of all Squared Errors

Limitation 2: Squared error penalises outliers very heavily. If one vendor had unusually low sales due to a holiday, the model may overfit to correct that one outlier instead of learning the general pattern.
Absolute value also makes all errors positive. But squaring has two advantages: (1) It's smooth everywhere — absolute value has a sharp corner at zero that makes calculus difficult. (2) It penalises large errors more — an error of 1000 counts as 10,00,000 (very bad), while an error of 10 counts as only 100. This encourages the machine to avoid large misses.
We can measure how wrong the machine is with the loss number. Now comes the key question: which way should we turn the knobs (weights)? The answer lies in calculus — specifically, in something called derivatives.
Concept 03

Derivatives — Which Way is Downhill?

A derivative tells you how sensitive one thing is to a small change in another. If you increase a weight by a tiny amount, does the loss go up or down, and by how much? That's the derivative of loss with respect to that weight.

You're hiking in the Nilgiri hills. You want to reach the lowest valley. Standing on a slope, you look around. The ground slopes steeply down to your left and gently upward to your right. That "steepness in each direction" is exactly what a derivative measures. Take a step in the direction of steepest descent — that's the machine learning algorithm.

For a function like f(x) = x², the derivative is 2x. This means: at x = 3, if you increase x a little, f(x) will increase by 6 times that little amount. So if the derivative is positive, we know increasing x makes things worse — so we should decrease x.

Partial Derivatives — When There Are Multiple Weights

What if we have two weights, w₁ and w₂? We ask: if we hold w₂ steady and nudge w₁ a tiny bit, how does the loss change? That's the partial derivative with respect to w₁. We do the same for w₂.

This gives us a direction for each weight independently. In our example:

∂Loss/∂w₁ = 6.25·w₁ + 7.375·w₂ − 26500
∂Loss/∂w₂ = 7.375·w₁ + 9.625·w₂ − 32750

At our starting weights (w₁=1000, w₂=3000):

∂Loss/∂w₁ = 6.25×1000 + 7.375×3000 − 26500 = +1875
∂Loss/∂w₂ = 7.375×1000 + 9.625×3000 − 32750 = +3500

Both are positive — meaning increasing either weight makes the loss go up. So we should decrease both weights.

Limitation: Partial derivatives only tell you the direction of steepest descent at the current point. The landscape of the loss surface can be complex — there may be many valleys (local minima), not just one. A derivative can guide you downhill but cannot guarantee you'll reach the lowest valley.
No! The intuition is all you need: a derivative tells you the direction and steepness of a slope at any point. In software, the computer calculates derivatives automatically (this is called "automatic differentiation"). You don't need to solve equations by hand. The key insight is: positive derivative → decrease the weight. Negative derivative → increase the weight.
Derivatives give us direction. But how big a step should we take? Too small and it takes forever. Too big and we might overshoot the best answer. The learning rate controls the step size, and this whole process of repeatedly stepping downhill is called gradient descent.
Concept 04

Gradient Descent — Hiking to the Valley

Gradient descent is the algorithm that uses derivatives to improve the weights, step by step. On each step, we calculate the derivative, then adjust each weight in the opposite direction:

New w₁ = Old w₁ − (Learning Rate × ∂Loss/∂w₁)
New w₂ = Old w₂ − (Learning Rate × ∂Loss/∂w₂)
🎯 Analogy Imagine you're blindfolded on a hillside in Kodaikanal and want to walk to the lowest point. You can feel the ground slope under your feet. You take a small step in whichever direction feels most downhill. Then you feel again, adjust direction if needed, and step again. After many steps, you reach the valley. That is gradient descent — a machine doing this millions of times every second.

The Learning Rate

The learning rate controls step size. Set at, say, 1% (0.01):

Updated w₁ = 1000 − (0.01 × 1875) = 981
Updated w₂ = 3000 − (0.01 × 3500) = 2965
Adjust the learning rate, then run gradient descent — watch the ball roll downhill!

⚠️ Limitations Local minima: The algorithm might find a valley that isn't the deepest one. On a complex loss surface (like in large neural networks), gradient descent may settle into a good-enough answer rather than the globally optimal one.

Learning rate sensitivity: Too large → the weights bounce wildly, loss explodes. Too small → training takes an impractically long time (millions of steps). Choosing the right learning rate is part science, part art.

Path is not straight: Even in our simple example, the weights don't move in a straight line toward the optimum — they curve. On more complex models, this winding path is far more dramatic.
Imagine a 3D terrain map where the height at each (w₁, w₂) point shows the loss. Our two-weight example looks like an elliptical bowl. The lowest point is the best weights. Gradient descent rolls a ball from wherever you start, and it follows the steepest slope downward. In real neural networks with billions of weights, this landscape exists in billions of dimensions — impossible to visualise, but mathematically the same idea.
We now have all the pieces: weights make predictions, loss measures the error, derivatives point the direction, and gradient descent takes the steps. Put it all together and you get the complete training loop — the actual process by which machines learn from data.
Concept 05

The Training Loop — Learning From Data

Training a model means running gradient descent many times — hundreds, thousands, or millions of iterations. Each time, the weights get slightly better. Over time, the model's predictions get closer to the true answers.

A student preparing for their Class 12 board exams in Tamil Nadu does practice papers (training data). They get feedback (loss). They figure out which topics need more work (derivatives). They study harder in those areas (gradient descent). After many practice cycles (training steps), they score better. The training loop is exactly this cycle, automated.

How Close Did We Get?

After 1000 steps the weights settle near w₁ ≈ 2311, w₂ ≈ 1633. The mathematically perfect (optimal) weights are w₁ = 2347, w₂ = 1604. We got very close!

With a smaller learning rate and more steps, we'd get even closer. In real machine learning, models train for millions of steps over enormous datasets.

Limitation 1: We used 3 data points and 2 weights. In general, if you have fewer data points than weights, the model can perfectly memorise the training data but fail on new data. This is called overfitting.

Limitation 2: Our model is linear — it can only draw a straight line through the data. To learn complex patterns (like language, images, speech), we need many layers and non-linear functions. But the training principle is identical: compute loss, compute gradients, update weights.

Limitation 3: The training data must be representative. If all our vendor examples are from one Chennai neighbourhood, the model may not generalise to vendors in Coimbatore or Madurai.
Everything we've covered here applies to real neural networks — they just do it at a vastly larger scale. Instead of 2 weights, GPT-style models have billions. Instead of 3 data points, they train on trillions of words. Instead of a simple linear equation, they use many layers of weighted sums followed by non-linear activation functions. But the core loop — predict, measure loss, compute derivatives, update weights — is the same principle at every level.

The Big Picture

Everything Connected

Every concept we explored is a station on the same railway line. The train starts at "random weights" and ends at "accurate predictions." Here's the complete journey:

🎲

Start: Random Weights

The machine begins with no knowledge. Weights are random — predictions will be wildly off.

⚙️

Step 1: Make Predictions

Multiply each input by its weight and add them up. The formula: Prediction = Σ(input × weight).

📏

Step 2: Calculate Loss

Measure how wrong each prediction is. Square the errors and add them — this gives the loss.

📐

Step 3: Compute Derivatives

Calculate partial derivatives — which direction should each weight move to reduce the loss?

🏃

Step 4: Update Weights (Gradient Descent)

Subtract (learning rate × derivative) from each weight. One step downhill on the loss surface.

🔄

Repeat → Converge

Go back to Step 1 with the new weights. Repeat thousands of times. Loss falls. Predictions improve.

🎯

End: Trained Model

The weights now encode what the machine has "learned" from the data. It can make good predictions on new inputs.
