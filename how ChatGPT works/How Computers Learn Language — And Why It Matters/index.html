Section 01

How does a basic spell checker work?

The very first spell checkers had one simple idea: keep a giant list of valid words. Check each word in a sentence against that list. If a word is missing from the list — flag it as a possible typo.

💡 Think of it like a bouncer at a club. The bouncer has a guest list. If your name is on it → you're in (✅ correct spelling). If not → you're flagged (❌ possible typo). The bouncer doesn't ask why you're there — just: are you on the list?

🤔 But what about "He walk to the store"?

Every word is in the dictionary — no red flags. The spell checker passes it. It cannot understand grammar or meaning; it only checks whether individual words exist. "Walk" is a real word, so the checker is satisfied. This is the limitation we need to fix.
Looking up individual words isn't enough. We need to look at pairs of words — and Tamil shows us exactly why.

Section 02

The Tamil Language Challenge

Tamil is an agglutinative language — suffixes are glued onto root words to change their meaning. The same root word appears in dozens of different forms. A simple dictionary cannot list every possible form, so a smarter approach is needed: look at which word combinations are natural.

💡 Think of it like Lego bricks. In English, words are mostly fixed shapes. In Tamil, you start with a root brick and snap suffixes onto it. The root "கடை" (kadai = shop) becomes an entirely different word depending on which suffix you attach:
கடை kadai shop (root)
கடைக்கு kadai + kku to the shop
கடையில் kadai + il in the shop
கடையிலிருந்து kadai + ilirundhu from the shop
கடைகள் kadai + kal shops (plural)

These are all the same root word. 👆 Click any card to see it used in a real Tamil sentence. A basic spell checker would need a separate dictionary entry for every single form — an impossible task. A smarter approach: learn which word pairs are natural from real Tamil text.

❌ Wrong pair (what's in the sentence)

வானம் + கடைக்கு

"வானம்" (vaanam = sky) + "கடைக்கு" (to the shop) → "the sky went to the shop." This word pair has never appeared in any Tamil text. The computer flags it immediately.

✅ Correct pair (what it should be)

அவன் + கடைக்கு

"அவன்" (avan = he) + "கடைக்கு" (to the shop) → a perfectly natural, common Tamil phrase seen thousands of times in everyday text.

💡 The key insight — pairs matter more than individual words

Collect large amounts of Tamil text and count every pair of adjacent words. If "அவன் கடைக்கு" appears thousands of times and "வானம் கடைக்கு" appears zero times — the computer knows something is wrong. No grammar rules written. Just counting. This is called a bigram model.
So how do we count all these word pairs systematically across an entire language?

Section 03

Build a Bigram Model

Let's build a bigram model step by step. We'll use a tiny made-up world where only 11 words exist — small enough to count by hand, but the logic is identical to what runs on your phone and inside large language models.

💡 Think of it like a tally chart. Every time you see two words sitting next to each other in the text, put a tally mark against that pair. After reading all the text, pairs with many tallies are "normal." Pairs with zero tallies? The model has never seen them — suspicious.
📖 What is a corpus? A corpus (plural: corpora) is simply a collection of text — the raw material a language model learns from. It could be a few sentences, a library of books, or the entire internet. The bigger and more varied the corpus, the better the model learns.

The Probability answers the question "Given the first word appeared, how likely is this the next word?" — for example, "He → went" at 75% means: every time "He" appears in this text, 75% of the time the very next word is "went." Probability = count of the pair ÷ count of the first word alone.

🔍 Test a sentence for errors

Try "He went the store" (has an error) or "He went to the store" (correct). The model catches errors purely through probability — not a single grammar rule was written.

⚠️ The limitation of bigrams: A bigram model only looks one word back. It can catch "went → the" as suspicious (probability 0%), but it cannot tell that "He no come home" is intentional dialogue in a story — understanding that requires remembering context from much earlier. This is where trigrams (looking 3 words back), then n-grams, and eventually transformer models come in. ChatGPT is a transformer — one that looks at the entire conversation at once. The bigram is its distant ancestor.

Section 04

Generate Text Like an AI

To check a sentence: look up a word pair in the table — if its probability is near zero, flag it. To generate a sentence: start with a word, look up the most probable next words, pick one, then repeat from the new word. It's the same table, used in the opposite direction.

💡 Think of it like following signposts. Each word is a town with signposts pointing to possible next towns, showing how likely each road is. You pick a road, arrive at the next town, and it has its own signposts. No map needed. No plan needed. Just follow the signs, one step at a time.

🔗 The connection to ChatGPT

ChatGPT does exactly this — but trained on hundreds of billions of words from the internet, books, and code. And instead of looking at just the one previous word, it looks at your entire conversation so far, using a mathematical structure called a transformer. The core idea, though, is the same seed planted by the bigram: predict the most probable next word, one step at a time, guided entirely by patterns in data.

At every step, the core act is the same: look at patterns in text, turn them into probabilities, and use those probabilities to predict what comes next. The only thing that changed from bigrams to ChatGPT is how much text was read, how far back the model looks, and how richly it represents what it learned.

🎯 The one thing to remember

Neither our tiny model nor ChatGPT truly "understands" language the way a human does. Both are doing the same thing at heart: making very fast, very educated guesses about what word comes next — based entirely on patterns learned from reading a lot of text. The difference is scale. Billions of parameters and trillions of words of training make those guesses so good they can feel like understanding.
