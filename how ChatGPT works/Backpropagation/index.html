Section 01

The Problem with Going Forward

Imagine you are training an AI model with one billion weights. A "weight" is just a number the model multiplies your input by — think of it like a dial on a machine that you can turn up or down. To teach the model, you need to know: for each dial, which direction should I turn it, and by how much?

To answer that, you need the partial derivative of the loss with respect to each weight. A partial derivative sounds scary, but it just means: "If I nudge this one dial a tiny bit, how much does the error change?"

The naïve approach: compute the answer for weight #1 (treat all others as frozen), then for weight #2, then for weight #3… and so on for a billion weights. Each calculation costs roughly the same effort as computing the error in the first place. Total cost: a billion times the cost of one forward pass. That is catastrophically slow.

The maze analogy. You have a maze with 1,000 entrances and one exit at the centre. If you start from each entrance one-by-one and trace to the centre, you repeat nearly all the work every time. But if you start from the centre and trace outward, you find all the paths in a single exploration — you share the common route from the centre across all paths.

Backpropagation does exactly this: it starts from the error (the centre) and traces backward to every weight simultaneously, sharing work along the way.

⚠ Limitations of derivatives in ML
  • Derivatives tell you the local slope — they cannot guarantee you find the global best answer, only improve from where you are.
  • If the loss landscape has many valleys, gradient descent can get trapped in a suboptimal valley (local minimum).
  • Partial derivatives assume smooth, differentiable operations. Some functions (like max(0,x)) have no derivative at exactly one point — this requires careful handling.

Now we know why we need a smarter approach. But how exactly does computing backwards save so much work? The key is a mathematical tool called the chain rule — which lets us multiply local effects together to get the global effect.

Section 02

The Chain Rule — Multiplying Local Effects

Suppose you have a two-step machine. Step A squares its input (x²). Step B cubes its input (x³). Feed 3 into A, get 9; feed 9 into B, get 729. If you nudge the original 3 a tiny bit, how much does 729 change? The chain rule answers this by multiplying the local sensitivity of each step together.

The courier relay analogy. Imagine a parcel moving through three couriers. The first courier is 6× faster than expected, so the parcel arrives at depot 2 six times earlier. From depot 2, the second courier is 243× faster. The total speed-up to the destination is 6 × 243 = 1,458×. Each courier only needs to know their own speed — they multiply, and the overall effect compounds.

Mathematically: if f(x) = (x²)³, the derivative is 6x⁵. At x = 3, that's 6 × 3⁵ = 1,458. Nudge 3 by 0.01 → output changes by ≈ 14.58. Let's verify that now.

⚠ What the chain rule cannot do
  • It requires every function in the chain to be differentiable at the point you evaluate it. Not all activation functions are (though workarounds exist).
  • In very deep chains (hundreds of steps), repeatedly multiplying small numbers makes the gradient shrink to near-zero — the vanishing gradient problem. Modern architectures work hard to avoid this.
  • Conversely, if numbers are large, gradients can explode — the network update becomes wildly unstable.

The chain rule gives us the mathematical backbone. Now let's apply it to a real model with weights, data, and a loss function — seeing both the forward and backward passes in action.

Section 03

The Forward Pass — Computing the Error

Before we can go backward, we must go forward. The forward pass is the model making its prediction and computing how wrong it is (the loss). Think of it as an accountant tallying up the bill before they figure out where the money went.

Let's use a simplified example: a model that predicts idli batter fermentation time (in hours) from two measurements — the temperature of the kitchen (in °C) and the ratio of urad dal to rice. The model uses two weights, w1 (temperature weight) and w2 (ratio weight).

The recipe dial analogy. Imagine you have two dials on a pressure cooker — one controls the heat (w1) and one controls the steam valve (w2). You run a batch of idlis and the cook time is wrong. Before you can fix the dials, you first need to measure how wrong the output was. That measurement is the loss. The forward pass is the cooking run; the backward pass is the diagnosis.

⚠ What the forward pass alone cannot tell you
  • The loss number tells you how wrong you are, not which weight to blame or which direction to adjust it.
  • Squaring the error (which is standard) means a prediction that's too high and one that's too low look equally bad — you still don't know the direction without backprop.
  • Loss computed on a small batch of examples may not represent how the model performs overall — this is the generalisation problem, a separate challenge.

We have the error. Now the real magic: we run the calculation backwards through each step, accumulating how much each weight contributed to that error. This is backpropagation.

Section 04

The Backward Pass — Tracing Blame

Now we run the same data backwards. Starting from the loss (a single number), we apply the chain rule at each step, multiplying local derivatives together as we flow toward each weight. The result at each weight is its gradient — the exact number that tells us how much the loss will change if we nudge that weight.

The blame-tracing analogy. A cricket match is lost by 5 runs. The coach doesn't just say "we lost" — they trace backward: the last over cost 14 runs, and in that over, one bowler conceded a wide that cost 3. Backpropagation does the same: it attributes a share of the total loss to each decision (weight) in the model, most-recent decisions first.

Why do multiple paths to the same weight get added?

If w1 flows through three separate data points (like three batches of idli batter), each one independently tells you how the loss would change. The total effect is the sum — because an increase in w1 simultaneously affects all three data points, and their effects on the loss stack up.

⚠ What backpropagation cannot guarantee
  • Backprop computes the gradient exactly, but using that gradient to update weights (gradient descent) still requires choosing a learning rate — too large and you overshoot, too small and training takes forever.
  • Backprop requires storing all intermediate values from the forward pass in memory. For very deep networks, this memory cost is enormous.
  • Backprop works backward through time in sequences, but very long sequences cause the vanishing/exploding gradient problem — this is one reason transformers replaced older recurrent networks.

We can compute the gradient. Now let's put it all together and watch a model actually learn — forward pass, gradients, weight update, and repeat.

Section 05

The Training Loop — Iterate Until Right

All the pieces come together in the training loop. It's a four-step cycle that repeats thousands or millions of times:

1. Run the forward pass → get the loss.  2. Run the backward pass → get the gradients.  3. Update each weight by subtracting a small fraction of its gradient.  4. Repeat.

The dosai temperature analogy. You're learning to make the perfect dosai. You cook one (forward pass), taste it and note how far off it is (loss). You think about which factor — tawa heat, batter thickness — was most responsible (backward pass). You adjust each dial slightly (weight update). You cook another dosai. Over many batches, you converge on the perfect settings — even if your first few attempts were terrible.

⚠ What can go wrong during training
  • Learning rate too high: The loss bounces around and never settles — you overshoot the minimum every time.
  • Learning rate too low: Training converges, but it takes so long it is impractical.
  • Overfitting: The model learns the training data so well that it fails on new data — it has memorised examples rather than learned general patterns.
  • Convergence to local minima: The gradient descent gets stuck in a local dip that is not the globally best solution.

Section 06

The Big Picture

Every concept we covered is a link in a single chain. Click each stop on the journey to expand it.

1

Weights & Loss

The model has dials (weights). The error is measured as loss.

The loss is a single number summarising how wrong the model's predictions are across all training examples. Minimising it is the entire goal of training. Without a clear error signal, there is nothing to learn from.
2

Partial Derivatives

For each weight, we ask: nudge it a tiny bit — does the loss go up or down, and by how much?

This is the partial derivative of the loss with respect to that weight. It gives us both the direction (positive = increase the loss, so go the other way) and the magnitude of the effect. Calculating this for billions of weights naïvely is catastrophically expensive.
3

The Chain Rule

Derivatives of chained functions multiply together — like compounding relay speeds.

The chain rule is the mathematical insight that makes backpropagation possible. It says: the derivative of the whole pipeline is the product of the derivative at each individual step. This lets us compute local sensitivities and combine them, rather than re-deriving everything from scratch for each weight.
4

Backpropagation

By starting from the loss and flowing backward, we compute ALL gradients in ~2× the cost of one forward pass.

Instead of computing each partial derivative separately (which would multiply the cost by the number of weights), backpropagation computes all gradients in a single backward sweep. It reuses intermediate results — like solving a maze from the centre. This is what made training large neural networks feasible.
5

Gradient Descent

Each weight is nudged slightly in the direction that reduces loss. Repeat thousands of times.

Gradient descent uses the computed gradients to update weights: new_weight = old_weight − learning_rate × gradient. The learning rate controls the step size. Over many iterations, the model gradually converges to weights that produce accurate predictions.
6

Training at Scale

The same loop — forward, backward, update — trains models with trillions of parameters.

Modern large language models use this exact training loop, applied to vast datasets with trillions of weights. The mathematics is identical to our simple two-weight example — only the scale differs. Specialised hardware (GPUs/TPUs) parallelises the forward and backward passes across thousands of cores simultaneously.
