How Machines Learn

A visual, interactive journey into the math behind Artificial Intelligence. Let's build a model to predict housing prices.

1

The Basic Machine

I want to explain the idea of "learning weights". We'll do it using an example where we can keep the numbers somewhat simple. We're going to build a model to predict home prices based on two factors.

Intuition Check: Imagine you have a soundboard with two volume knobs. One knob controls the bass, one controls the treble. By turning them, you change the final audio output. Our "machine" has two inputs (House Size and Lot Size), and it gives one output: a Predicted Price.

Without thinking too hard, it seems like we could multiply the house size by some number, multiply the lot size by some other number, and add those together to get our prediction. Let's call these numbers weights: w₁ and w₂.

House Size

× w₁ (Weight 1)
Input 2

Lot Size

× w₂ (Weight 2)
→
Output

Predicted Price

(Size × w₁) + (Lot × w₂)

3

The First Guess & Measuring Error

People figured out the math for picking the best possible w₁ and w₂ hundreds of years ago. However, we're not going to worry about that. Instead we'll purposely go about it in a way that will seem overcomplicated and expensive to compute because this technique will prove magical for modern AI.

Forget about finding the ideal weights right now; just pick some random numbers. Let's pick w₁ = 1000 and w₂ = 3000.

Calculating the Error

Error = Predicted Price - Actual Price

House 1: (1 × 1000) + (1.5 × 3000) = 5500 Error: 5500 - 5000 = +500
House 2: (0.75 × 1000) + (1.25 × 3000) = 4500 Error: 4500 - 3500 = +1000
House 3: (1.25 × 1000) + (1 × 3000) = 4250 Error: 4250 - 4500 = -250

Wow! Not too bad for a random guess. But if we pick new weights that improve the prediction for house 1 but make it much worse for house 2, that wouldn't be so great. We need a single number that says how good our prediction is across all the houses.

4

Calculating "Loss"

If we just add the errors together, a prediction that is 1000 too high (+1000) and another that is 1000 too low (-1000) will equal zero. The machine would think it's perfect, even though both guesses were terrible.

We could take the absolute value, but instead we are going to square the errors. Squaring takes negative numbers and makes them positive, treating a mistake of -250 the exact same as a mistake of +250. It also has a secret advantage: it disproportionately punishes big mistakes.

Loss is the sum of the squared errors across all the houses. Think of it as how much we are losing in our predictions. The less, the better.

  • House 1: (500)² = 250,000
  • House 2: (1000)² = 1,000,000
  • House 3: (-250)² = 62,500

Our starting Loss with random weights is 1,312,500. This single Loss number is a beautiful thing. If we tweak our weights and the loss goes down, we're moving in the right direction.

5

The Magic of Calculus (Expanding the Math)

If we increase w₁ a little bit, will the loss go up (bad) or down (good)? What about w₂? That type of question might sound familiar from high school calculus.

Calculus Refresher: Remember the function f(x) = x²? The derivative is 2x.
If x = 3, the derivative is 2(3) = 6. This means if we increase x by a tiny amount (like 0.01), f(x) will increase by exactly 6 times that amount (0.06). The derivative tells us the slope.

Since we want to compute derivatives of our Loss, let's actually write out the entire Loss calculation as a giant algebra equation using variables instead of hardcoded numbers.

// Total Loss = (House 1 Error)² + (House 2 Error)² + (House 3 Error)²

Loss =
(5000 - (1*w₁ + 1.5*w₂))² +
(3500 - (0.75*w₁ + 1.25*w₂))² +
(4500 - (1.25*w₁ + 1*w₂))²

If you multiply that all out by hand and combine like terms (or ask an online calculator to do it), you get this beast of a polynomial equation:

Loss = 3.125(w₁)² + 7.375(w₁)(w₂) - 26500(w₁) + 4.8125(w₂)² - 32750(w₂) + 57500000

We want the derivative of the loss, but with respect to what? w₁ or w₂? We care about both! Here we cross into a concept called the Partial Derivative. Think of this like: if we hold w₂ completely steady like a constant number, and we increase w₁ a tiny bit, how much will the loss increase or decrease?

Check Your Understanding

If we treat w₂ as a constant, what happens to the term 4.8125(w₂)² when we take the partial derivative with respect to w₁?

Using basic calculus power rules (x² → 2x and cx → c), taking the partial derivative of that long polynomial gives us two beautifully simple equations for our slopes:

Slope for w₁

6.25(w₁) + 7.375(w₂) - 26500
(The 6.25 comes from 2 × 3.125)
At w₁=1000, w₂=3000:
6.25(1k) + 7.375(3k) - 26500 = 1875

Slope for w₂

7.375(w₁) + 9.625(w₂) - 32750
(The 9.625 comes from 2 × 4.8125)
At w₁=1000, w₂=3000:
7.375(1k) + 9.625(3k) - 32750 = 3500

If we increase w₁ a little bit, Loss will go up by around 1875 times that little bit. So we should decrease w₁. Same for w₂. Now we know exactly which direction to move to find the bottom of the valley.

6

Taking a Step (Learning Rate)

We know the direction to move, but how big of an adjustment do we make? If we adjust by a very small amount, the loss will barely change. If we adjust by a huge amount, we might overshoot the optimal value.

We assume a Learning Rate which we'll set to 1% (0.01). We adjust each weight by subtracting the partial derivative multiplied by the learning rate.

Updated w₁ = 1000 - (0.01 × 1875) = 981
Updated w₂ = 3000 - (0.01 × 3500) = 2965

Why do we multiply by the derivative instead of just moving a flat 10 units every time? Because we want to take larger jumps when w₁ and w₂ are far away from optimal (where the slope is steep), and smaller jumps when we get closer to the flat bottom of the valley.

7

Journey to the Bottom

Imagine you were placed somewhere in Death Valley National Park and your goal was to hike to the lowest point in the park: Badwater Basin. If the park were shaped like a perfectly round cereal bowl, no matter where you started, you could just step in the steepest direction and walk in a straight line to the bottom.

But our Loss surface is shaped like an elliptical bowl. To move in the steepest direction, you must cross the contour lines perpendicularly. Your path isn't going to be straight.

Conclusion

We are only off by 22 on house #3! You may be itching to know how close we got to the optimal answer. People figured out how to solve these equations algebraically a long time ago without doing 1000 steps of computation. If you set both partial derivatives to 0 and solve for w₁ and w₂, you get:

w₁ = 2347   |   w₂ = 1604

We got incredibly close (w₁ ≈ 2311, w₂ ≈ 1633). Through the simple iterative process of guessing, measuring loss, calculating the slopes, and stepping forward, our machine literally taught itself to find the bottom of the valley.
