The Basic Machine

Welcome to the core concept of machine learning! We are going to build a model—think of it as a machine—to predict housing prices.

Intuition Check: Imagine you have a soundboard with two volume knobs. One knob controls the bass, one controls the treble. By turning them, you change the final audio output. Our "machine" has two inputs (House Size and Lot Size), and it gives one output: a Predicted Price.

Without overthinking it, a simple way to do this is to multiply the house size by some number, multiply the lot size by another number, and add them together. Let's call these multiplier numbers the weights of our model: w₁ and w₂.

Input 1

House Size

× w₁ (Weight 1)
Input 2

Lot Size

× w₂ (Weight 2)
→
Output

Predicted Price

(Size × w₁) + (Lot × w₂)

The First Guess & Measuring Error

Hundreds of years ago, mathematicians figured out exact algebra to calculate the perfect weights. But we are going to do it the hard, iterative way, because this "guess and adjust" technique is the exact mechanism that proves magical for modern AI (like Neural Networks).

Forget finding the perfect weights right now; let's just pick random numbers. We'll start with our volume knobs turned to w₁ = 1000 and w₂ = 3000.

Calculating the Error

Error = Predicted Price - Actual Price

House 1: (1 × 1000) + (1.5 × 3000) = 5500 Error: 5500 - 5000 = +500
House 2: (0.75 × 1000) + (1.25 × 3000) = 4500 Error: 4500 - 3500 = +1000
House 3: (1.25 × 1000) + (1 × 3000) = 4250 Error: 4250 - 4500 = -250
4

Calculating "Loss"

Check Your Understanding

Why can't we just add the errors (+500, +1000, -250) together to get our total accuracy score?

To fix the cancellation problem, we square the errors. Squaring turns negative numbers positive, treating a mistake of -250 the exact same as a mistake of +250.

Alternative Perspective: Think of Loss as a "Penalty Score" in a video game. Squaring the error also disproportionately punishes big mistakes. Being off by 1000 isn't just twice as bad as being off by 500; squared, it's four times as bad! The machine will prioritize fixing its biggest blunders first.

Loss Calculation (w₁=1000, w₂=3000)

  • House 1: (500)² = 250,000
  • House 2: (1000)² = 1,000,000
  • House 3: (-250)² = 62,500
Total Loss (Sum of squared errors): 1,312,500

The Magic of Calculus (Derivatives)

If we turn our w₁ knob up slightly, will our Loss go up (bad) or down (good)? To find out, we use high school calculus—specifically, the derivative.

Gut-Level Intuition: Imagine you are blindfolded, standing on the side of a hill. You can't see the bottom, but you can feel the slope of the ground beneath your feet. The derivative is simply your foot telling you, "The ground is sloping UP in front of you, and DOWN behind you."

Because we have two weights to adjust (w₁ and w₂), we use a partial derivative. This just means we freeze time, keep w₂ completely still, and feel the slope only in the w₁ direction.

Taking a Step (Learning Rate)

We know the direction to move (decrease), but how big of a step should we take? If we take a massive leap, we might overshoot the valley floor entirely and end up higher on the opposite mountain. If we take microscopic baby steps, it will take centuries to get to the bottom.

We control this step size using a Learning Rate. Let's set it to 1% (0.01). We update our weights using this beautiful, simple rule:

New Weight = Old Weight - (Learning Rate × Derivative)

Why multiply by the derivative? It's a brilliant math trick! When we are high up on the mountain, the slope (derivative) is very steep (a large number), so we take a large jump. As we get closer to the flat bottom of the valley, the slope gets closer to zero, so our steps automatically get smaller, preventing us from overshooting!
